The System of Intent

The enterprise has run on a system of record for fifty years. I think its successor won't record what happened — it will understand what everyone wants, and act on it. I call that surface Karpa*. This is my attempt to describe it before it exists.

* Karpa — from the Sanskrit karpāsa, the woven cotton cloth that is the distant root of the English word canvas (karpāsa → Greek kárpasos → Latin carbasus → canvas).

By Srinivas Chilukuri Personal views ~34 min read
Abstract

For half a century the enterprise has been built around a system of record — and we mistook a workaround for an architecture. The database was the scarce resource, so we shaped the company to fit it: normalized tables, rigid schemas, functional modules, overnight batch runs, and a discipline of master data to keep the record clean. ERP was never a theory of how companies should work. It was a workaround for what computers could not yet do.

Those limits are gone. Storage is effectively free, machines read language and images natively, and reasoning is abundant and cheap. I argue that the supply chain — the most cross-functional, most constrained, most disruption-exposed system in the manufacturing enterprise — is where the record-keeping era should end first. In its place I propose a System of Intent, built on a shared surface I call Karpa: a place where any participant — customer, planner, plant, supplier, financier — expresses what they want in whatever form is natural, and where AI interprets, reconciles, orchestrates, and acts. Structure becomes an output rendered on demand, not a cage imposed on the way in. What follows is the mechanism, the architecture, how it handles the unforgiving cases of master data and control, an end-to-end operating model, a resilience case from pharmaceutical sourcing, the honest objections, and a staged path from today's ERP to an intent-native operation.

I — The provocation

ERP is a product of its time

Every sacred feature of the enterprise operating system was an engineering compromise forced by the constraints of its birth. We have mistaken those compromises for the nature of business itself.

Consider what a computer could not do in 1985, and watch each limitation harden into something we now defend as a principle. The relational database was a triumph of scarcity management. Disk was expensive, so you stored every fact exactly once and forbade redundancy. Memory was small, so you processed in batches overnight. Machines could not read a sentence, so a human had to pre-digest every intention into codes, fields, and forms before the system could touch it. Integration between systems was costly and brittle, so vendors bundled everything into a single monolith with one shared schema — and called the compartments inside it modules. And software could not exercise judgment, so it was confined to recording and reporting while humans decided. We built an entire profession to feed the record and another to read it.

  • Storage was expensiveStore each fact once; forbid redundancy. The normalized schema was born — and with it, the requirement to decide the shape of the world before you capture anything in it. The rule could not even hold on its own terms: reading those facts back was slow, so redundancy crept straight back in — indexes, cached and materialized views, summary tables, a whole parallel data warehouse — duplicating the very facts the schema was built to keep single.
  • Machines could not read languageHumans pre-digest meaning into codes and fields. The form, the dropdown, and the transaction became the only doors into the system.
  • Compute was scarce and serialYou could not re-optimize continuously, so you batched. Overnight MRP, weekly S&OP, the monthly close — cadence dictated by the machine, not the business.
  • Integration was costly and brittleBundle everything behind one schema. The ERP suite, and the functional silos inside it, were a way to avoid the cost of systems talking to each other.
  • Software could not judgeThe system records; the human decides. "System of record" was not an aspiration. It was a ceiling.

Each of these was the correct decision in 1985. Each has since calcified into something we treat as permanent. We say a company "has silos" as though silos were a cultural failing to be fixed with workshops. They are not a culture. They are an artifact of a schema. The planner and the finance analyst disagree because they are looking at two projections of the same reality that the software was never able to hold in one place. Remove the constraint that created the compartments, and the compartments have no reason to exist.

A thought experiment

Suppose history had run in the other order — that machines could always read any sentence, document, and image, and only then did someone propose the relational database, the mandatory form, and the module. Would we have built them at all? We would have seen them for what they are: elaborate machinery for a problem that no longer exists. ERP is not wrong. It is the right answer to a question we have stopped being asked.

II — What changed

The constraints are gone

Four capabilities, now ordinary, dissolve the four compromises one for one.

  • Storage is free and unstructured data is first-class. You no longer decide the schema before you capture the fact. Capture first; derive structure later, as many times and as many ways as you need.
  • Models read any modality. An email, a photograph of a damaged pallet, a supplier's PDF, a voice note on the plant floor — the machine extracts the meaning. The human no longer has to translate intent into the system's vocabulary.
  • Reasoning compute is abundant. Continuous re-optimization replaces the batch. The plan is not a document produced on Sunday night; it is a living state that updates whenever intent or the world changes.
  • Interpretation replaces integration. This is the quiet one, and the most consequential. The monolith existed to spare us the cost of systems agreeing on meaning. A model that reads meaning mediates between any two systems without a shared schema. The reason to bundle everything into one suite evaporates.

And the fifth, which sits above the other four: software can now exercise judgment. It can weigh options, plan across steps, act through tools, and explain itself. The ceiling called "system of record" is lifted.

The tempting half-measure. The obvious response to this capability shift is to place agents on top of the existing stack — a decision layer above the APS and the ERP. That captures real value, and fast. But it preserves the very schema the agents spend their cycles working around. If the constraints that created the schema are gone, the higher-value move is not to decorate the record. It is to retire its primacy.

The gap between what companies invest in AI and the value they get back is not a model problem. It is a paradigm problem: capable agents are being asked to operate inside an architecture built to deny them the thing they are best at — reading the world as it actually arrives.

A system of record stores what happened. A System of Intent holds what everyone wants — and continuously turns it into what happens next.

The thesis, in one line

III — The thesis

From record to intent

The industry already has half of the vocabulary. We moved from the System of Record — ERP, the ledger of what happened — to the System of Engagement — CRM, portals, mobile, the layer of how we interact. The third member of the family has been missing. It is the System of Intent: the layer that holds what every participant wants, needs, promises, and observes, and turns that raw material of coordination directly into action.

A System of Record stores transactions. A System of Intent holds intentions, constraints, commitments, and signals — and continuously composes them into action. The ledger still exists. Auditors, regulators, and tax authorities still need it. But it is demoted from master to projection: one of the views the system can render on demand, rather than the mold every input must be forced into.

I call the surface Karpa — the canvas of world business. The name goes to the source: our word canvas descends through the Latin carbasus and the Greek kárpasos from the Sanskrit karpāsa, the woven cotton cloth. Karpa is that cloth made digital — a single, boundless surface anyone can write on, in any form, onto which the scattered intent of everyone a supply chain touches, inside the company and beyond it, is woven into one coherent, living picture of what should happen next.

IV — The mechanism

The five inversions

The transformer did not make recurrence faster; it removed it. The System of Intent does the same to five assumptions we stopped questioning. Each inversion turns a constraint of scarcity into a capability of abundance — and all five follow from a single idea.

The Intent Principle

In any system that can interpret meaning, structure is an output, not an input.

The moment a machine can read what you mean, every form, schema, and module built to make meaning machine-legible becomes optional — and what is optional in an operating system eventually becomes overhead.

V — The architecture

Anatomy of Karpa

Karpa is not an app and not a chatbot bolted to an ERP. It is a thin, opinionated stack with six layers. Read it left to right as the life of a single intent — from the moment someone expresses it to the moment the world has moved.

1 · The Surface — capture, as-is

Anyone a supply chain touches writes to Karpa in whatever form is natural. A hospital group emails, "We need 500 more units for the Lagos launch; we can move the date a week if it lowers cost." A line supervisor leaves a voice note. A supplier uploads a certificate of analysis as a PDF. A sensor posts a temperature excursion. There are no mandatory fields and no training course on screens. The cost of participating drops to the cost of saying what you mean.

2 · Memory — the unstructured store

Everything is retained in its original form, embedded for retrieval, time-stamped, and versioned. Nothing is discarded because it did not fit a column. Managing unstructured data at scale is exactly the capability enterprises have long lacked — and exactly the layer that becomes an asset once interpretation is cheap. Memory is not a swamp; it is the single source of context the rest of the stack reasons over.

3 · Interpretation — meaning, extracted

The interpreter continuously turns raw memory into four structured primitives: intent (what someone wants), constraints (what must hold — a regulatory limit, a line capacity, a credit cap), commitments (what has been promised, by whom, by when), and signals (what the world is doing). These four are the vocabulary of coordination. Crucially, interpretation is what lets Karpa speak to legacy systems without a shared schema — it reads SAP's meaning and a supplier's spreadsheet alike.

4 · Orchestration — the agent mesh

A mesh of agents plans across the formerly-siloed domains, negotiates trade-offs between revenue, cost, service, and risk, and acts through tools — placing an order, re-sequencing a line, releasing inventory, drafting a supplier message. This is where the effectiveness-versus-efficiency trade-off dissolves: optimization becomes continuous and granular rather than a weekly committee. High-stakes moves are routed to people; the rest resolve themselves.

5 · Projection — structure on demand

When a human or a system needs structure, Karpa renders it: a purchase order, an MRP plan, a journal entry, a regulatory filing, a board-ready dashboard. The projection is validated against the constraints, so it is not a looser artifact than today's — it is a tighter one, generated with its full lineage attached. ERP, where it survives, becomes a projection target.

6 · Trust — the spine, not the gate

Every interpretation and every action carries its provenance: which inputs, which reasoning, which confidence, which human approved what. Decision rights and confidence thresholds decide what acts autonomously and what waits for a person. Transparent, auditable, explainable decisions are treated not as a compliance afterthought but as the load-bearing spine. Karpa is more auditable than an ERP, because the ledger only ever told you what changed, never why.

And the one everyone asks for: Ask-anything

Because all intent and context live in one interpretable place, every participant gets a conversational way in. "Why did my order slip?" returns the actual causal chain, not a status code. "What if the API from our Chinese site drops 30% next quarter?" runs a simulation across the mesh and shows the consequences before they happen. The chat is not a feature bolted on top. It is the natural interface to a surface that already understands.

Karpa you can touch

Everything above is an architecture — but Karpa is also, literally, a surface, and the furthest edge of this vision is that it becomes a real product: Karpa surfaces, everywhere people already are. Picture a writable panel on the shopfloor wall where a supervisor scrawls a note or sketches a line change, and it reads the handwriting, the diagram, the arrow, and acts on it. Picture a screen on the machine itself, a tablet on a warehouse rack, a panel a sales rep hands across the table to a customer, a browser tab a supplier signs into. It is one Karpa, meeting each person in the medium they already use — pen, voice, keyboard, photo, upload. There is no enterprise application to log into and navigate; there is a surface you write on, and it understands. The panel on the wall, the screen on the machine, the tablet in the clinic, and the browser tab on the planner's laptop are all windows onto the same living Karpa. This is the part that looks most like science fiction today and will feel most obvious in a decade: the interface to the enterprise stops being software you operate and becomes a surface you talk to.

VI — The hard cases

Master data, the BOM, and the life of a product

A manufacturing reader should stop me here, and the objection is the right one. Supply chains run on precise, unforgiving structure: a master record that says exactly which product this is, a bill of materials that must be correct to the component, a product definition that tooling and regulators depend on. If structure is an output rendered on demand, what happens to the structure you cannot afford to get wrong?

Structure as an output does not mean structure is soft. Some projections are ephemeral — a dashboard, an answer to a question — recomputed each time they are asked for. Others are pinned: rendered once, validated hard against every constraint, approved by the accountable human, then frozen with their full lineage and treated as immutable until deliberately superseded. A master record, a released BOM, a batch record, a regulatory filing are the second kind. Karpa captures loosely and releases strictly. What changes is not the rigour of the artifact — it is where it comes from, and what it stays connected to.

Master data — identity resolved, not decreed

Today, master data is a golden record: one canonical row, governed centrally, that every input must match before it is let in. The cost is the master-data program that never ends — deduplication, reconciliation, the permanent battle for one version of the truth.

On Karpa, identity is resolved, not decreed. Every time anyone refers to a product — a customer's email, a supplier's certificate of analysis, an engineering drawing, a photograph of the carton — the Interpretation layer links that mention to a persistent entity, the way a person recognises "the 50 mg paediatric vial" across a dozen ways of naming it. The entity accretes everything ever said about it: every part number and synonym, every image, every specification, every market it is approved in. The canonical record still exists — but as a governed projection over that memory, rendered and continuously reconciled, not a parallel table kept clean by hand. The never-ending cleanup becomes a standing property: contradictions surface the moment they appear, and the Trust layer decides which assertions are authoritative, with the safety-critical fields gated behind human sign-off.

This is also what finally fixes ordering the right thing. Variants and versions are the bane of the SKU table — a flat explosion of near-identical codes nobody can keep straight. Here the product is one living entity with a version history and a variant tree. When a customer orders ambiguously — "500 of the Lagos vial" — the system disambiguates from context (their market, their regulatory approvals, their order history) and closes the loop the way a good counter clerk does: it shows back the resolved part number, a picture, and the spec, and asks for confirmation when its confidence is below threshold. The image and the number are not bolted on; they are there because the surface captured them in the first place. Ordering stops being a form you must already know the code to complete, and becomes a short exchange that ends in certainty.

The bill of materials — generated, validated, pinned

The BOM is the sharpest test, because a BOM must be exactly right — a wrong component is a recall, or worse. So let me be unambiguous: on Karpa the BOM is not fuzzy, and it is not re-interpreted at the point of use. It is a pinned projection — assembled from the authoritative engineering intent in memory, validated against every constraint (regulatory, material, tolerance, supplier qualification), approved by the responsible engineer, then frozen and signed. It is as rigorous as any BOM released today — more so, because it carries its full lineage: which requirement, which approval, which qualified material, and why.

What changes is maintenance. Because the released BOM stays linked to the intent and constraints that produced it, a change anywhere upstream — a component goes obsolete, a tolerance tightens, a supplier loses a certificate — is no longer a manual hunt for every affected assembly. The system flags every BOM that depends on the changed input and proposes the delta for approval. The engineering-change chase that eats weeks across disconnected systems becomes a reconciliation the surface runs continuously and a human ratifies. And because the BOM is a projection rather than a prisoner of one PLM or ERP schema, memory can render it into whatever the plant, the MES, or a supplier actually consumes — without an integration project between each pair of systems.

PLM — the product as a living entity

New-product introduction is where the silo tax is highest: marketing's intent, engineering's constraints, the plant's manufacturability, and suppliers' commitments live in different systems and meet too late — at launch, when the conflicts are most expensive to resolve. Traditional PLM manages this as a sequence of controlled documents handed between those silos through stage gates.

The futuristic version is easier to state than to build, but it follows directly from everything above: the product is a living entity in memory from the first conversation about it — the earliest "what if we made this," the voice of the customer, the regulatory landscape, the first CAD sketch, the plant's producibility feedback, the supplier quotes — all accreting around one entity on one surface. The lifecycle is not a relay of documents; it is the accumulation of intent, constraints, commitments, and signals around that entity over time. Stage-gate reviews still happen, but the gate packet is a projection rendered on demand, not the primary reality. Conflicts surface early — "no qualified supplier can hold this tolerance inside your lead-time window" — instead of at launch. "Launch" stops being a status flag flipped in a database and becomes the moment the product's definition is pinned and released to the systems that need it. And because nothing is discarded, the product's entire genealogy — why every decision was made — stays queryable for the life of the product. Ask-anything across a decade of a product's life is what makes the next recall, audit, or redesign cheap: the reasoning was never thrown away.

None of this is a loosening. It is the same discipline — generated instead of hand-maintained, and kept connected to the intent it came from, rather than frozen into a schema and left to go quietly stale.

VII — Access & authority

Who sees what, and who decides

Put everything a company knows onto one interpretable surface and you have just described a security officer's nightmare: the richest target in the enterprise, in one place, readable by a machine. This does not weaken the case; it raises the stakes on access control and makes it load-bearing. Encryption, isolation, and zero-trust networking are table stakes — assume them. The interesting question is the one you asked: pricing cannot be visible to everyone, suppliers must never see each other's terms, and someone, somewhere, still has to approve. How does that work when there is no screen to lock and no table to put a permission flag on?

Permission attaches to meaning, not to the schema. In the record paradigm, your access is bolted to structure — the tables, columns, and screens you are allowed to touch, maintained as an entitlement matrix that, like master data, is never finished. On an interpretable surface, access is a policy stated in intent — "a supplier sees only their own terms"; "margin is visible to the account owner, not the planner" — and it is enforced at the moment a view is rendered. Because structure is produced on demand, so is disclosure.

Access — enforced at projection, not pre-granted

Nothing is shown from a raw store. Every view is a projection, composed for the person or agent asking — and the same policies that validate a projection also filter it. Ask for anything touching price, and what you get back is assembled to your entitlement: the customer sees their own price; a competitor's buyer never does; an internal planner sees cost-to-serve but not customer-specific margin unless they own the relationship; a supplier sees only its own terms, never a rival's. One body of memory, many governed projections. This is finer-grained than role-based table permissions, not coarser — policy can turn on context a column grant cannot express — and it is more auditable, because every disclosure carries provenance: who saw what, when, and under which rule. The honest cost is that the policy layer becomes critical infrastructure: it must be written carefully, reviewed, and tested like code, because it is now the thing standing between a question and an answer.

Roles — defined by judgment, not by access

Many of today's roles are defined by the system: the AP clerk, the expeditor, the master-data steward, the buyer who exists partly to operate a screen. A large share of those roles exist to feed and reconcile the record — and that is exactly the work the surface absorbs. They do not vanish so much as transform. Access and authority decouple from the software: your role stops being "what the system lets you touch" and becomes "what you are trusted to decide and accountable to answer for."

That shift creates roles that barely exist today and elevates a few that do. Policy authors write and own the access and decision rules the agents enforce. Exception owners handle what the agents escalate — the hard, novel, high-consequence calls. System and agent designers shape the rules the mesh runs under. And every pinned artifact — a released BOM, a batch record, a dual-source activation — has a named accountability holder who signs it. The functional silos dissolve as data silos, because they were an artifact of the schema; but human expertise domains remain. A quality lead still owns quality judgment. What changes is that their authority is expressed as decision rights on the surface, not as a login to a quality module.

Approval — moved up, not removed

Someone still approves — but not every transaction, one at a time. Approval splits in two. Routine, high-confidence decisions inside pre-authorised bounds are exercised by agents directly: the approval was granted in advance, as policy, and every action it covers is logged with its full rationale for audit. That is the "behind the scenes" you intuited — not an absence of approval, but approval pre-expressed as the bounds agents operate within. Consequential, novel, or threshold-exceeding decisions are routed to the accountable human with the reasoning, the simulation, and the audit trail already attached — as the dual-source activation is routed to the quality lead in the scenario that follows. The human decides faster, because the agent has done the work of framing the decision rather than burying it in a queue.

So the approval hierarchy does not disappear; it becomes a decision-rights graph — who may decide what, at what confidence, within which spend and risk ceilings — that agents enforce continuously and leaders adjust. Humans approve policies and exceptions; agents execute within them. The safeguard against over-automation is built into the sequence the whole architecture follows: thresholds start conservative and widen only as trust is earned. Autonomy is granted, audited, and tightened — never assumed.

A worked example: who signs the purchase order?

Take the case most people reach for. Today a purchase analyst creates the PO and a purchase manager approves it before release. That split is not workflow for its own sake — it is a control: separation of duties, so that no single person can both originate a commitment and authorise it. It exists because people err and occasionally defraud, and because the record is the only evidence, so you need a second human to vouch for the first. Spending thresholds and delegation-of-authority matrices are simply a way to ration a scarce resource — trustworthy attention — by the size of the risk.

When an agent can compose the PO, "two humans in sequence" no longer fits — but its purpose matters more than ever, because an agent acting at scale can also err at scale. So separation of duties should not be deleted; it should be reborn in a stronger form and moved to where it belongs. The analyst-and-manager pair becomes three concerns, held apart on purpose:

  • Act. An execution agent composes the PO from the intent, the contract, and the live constraints, and proposes it — acting under a scoped, time-boxed authority granted for that one task, not a standing role login.
  • Verify. An independent control agent — a different owner and a different objective, so it cannot be captured by the agent that acts — checks every PO against policy before release. Not a tired manager sampling a queue, but a full check of every line, un-bribable and fully logged.
  • Own. A named human holds accountability — but not by clicking approve on each PO. They authored the buying policy the agents enforce, and they own the exception queue and the outcome. One person now stands behind thousands of orders by standing behind the policy and the exceptions, not the keystrokes.

Two things follow. First, most of today's complexity was accidental — approval tiers, out-of-office re-routing, the delegation-of-authority spreadsheet — machinery built to ration human attention, and all of it can go. What remains is essential: no single actor may act and vouch for itself; verification must be independent; a human must own the outcome. Simpler at the transaction, stronger in control. Second, the separation that used to run between two clerks now runs at the policy layer: whoever sets execution policy must not be whoever sets control policy, and a third function owns the audit. Fraud risk moves from "two people collude over an order" to "someone quietly changes the rules" — so the crown jewels become change-control over policy and the integrity of the provenance trail. Guard those two, and the back-office ledger of approvals collapses into a line of policy and a continuous, automatic proof that it was followed.

But the most out-of-the-box version of this question is whether the purchase order should exist at all. For the routine case — raw material for a product you already make, against demand you have already committed to — the PO is itself a fossil: a discrete, approved document that survives because two companies ran separate systems and needed a handshake artifact, and because a financial commitment needed a human gate. Dissolve the fragmentation and the artifact loses its reason to exist. What the business actually wants is not a purchase order; it is the right material, in the right place, at the right time, within agreed terms, fully traceable. Express that as a standing intent — "keep the line supplied to meet committed demand, within this price formula and these quality specs, from these qualified suppliers" — agree the commercial frame with the supplier once, and replenishment simply flows. There is no create-then-approve, because there is no fresh decision to gate: each order is the execution of an agreement already made, not a new commitment. The human judgment does not vanish; it moves up to where it was always most valuable — setting and adjusting the frame, and owning the exceptions when reality steps outside it. Even the three-way match dissolves: on one surface, the commitment, the goods receipt, and the invoice are three projections of a single reconciled reality, so there is nothing left to match. The act–verify–own structure above is then what remains for the decisions that are genuinely new — a fresh supplier, a renegotiation, a spike beyond the formula — not for the ninety-odd percent of ordering that should quietly happen on its own. The deepest simplification is not a better approval workflow. It is noticing that most of what we approve are transactions we only invented because the machine could not be trusted to mean what we meant.

IX — The operating model

From molecule to medicine, end to end

The sections so far describe a mechanism. It helps to see the whole operating model at once — the path a pharmaceutical company takes from a molecule to a delivered medicine — and to set today's flow beside the one I am proposing. Today's is a relay: each function owns a system, and work is handed across boundaries where data is re-keyed, reconciled, and delayed. On Karpa the same functions write to one surface; agents interpret and act within policy; and people move from operating systems to owning decisions.

Read the right-hand column as a career map as much as a process one. The roles that exist to feed and reconcile the record — sales ops re-keying orders, planners firefighting in spreadsheets, stewards cleaning master data, reviewers chasing documents — thin out. The roles that grow are the ones software cannot hold: deciding under ambiguity, owning an outcome, authoring the policy the agents run under, and handling the exception no rule anticipated. Fewer people feeding the machine; more people pointing it.

X — Resilience

A property, not a project

Supply chain resilience today is a binder. Someone maps the network once, qualifies a second supplier on paper, and files it. But as one sourcing leader puts it, "a material may have several qualified suppliers on paper, but that does not necessarily translate into genuine supply resilience." The map is stale the day it is finished, and the disruption is discovered in the crisis, not before it.

On Karpa, resilience stops being a project and becomes a standing property of the surface. Because every tier, supplier signal, constraint, and scenario already lives in one interpretable place, the system does continuously what the binder did once:

  • Sees past tier one. Most companies lack visibility beyond their direct suppliers; Karpa reads certificates, filings, and trade data to surface the single-source dependency two and three tiers deep — the hidden node that actually fails.
  • Keeps readiness live. A qualified alternate is not a document; it is a state Karpa maintains and re-checks — lead time, regulatory status, capacity — so it can be activated in hours, not quarters.
  • Simulates before the shock. "What if this region faces a trade restriction?" is a continuous background question, not a workshop. The performance envelope now includes resilience without trading away cost.

XI — The honest objections

"This looks dystopian"

It should, from here. So did removing the ledger clerk, the typing pool, and the mainframe operator. A vision worth having has to survive its hardest objections, so here are the five I come back to most.

"AI hallucinates — you can't let it run operations."

You don't let it run everything, and not at once. Decision rights and confidence thresholds decide what acts autonomously and what waits for a person; high-stakes moves route to humans by design, as in the scenario above. And Karpa is more controllable than the status quo, not less: every action carries the intent, evidence, and reasoning that produced it. Today's ERP will tell you a number changed; it will never tell you why. Auditability is the feature, not the risk.

"Unstructured data is ungovernable."

Structure-on-read is not the absence of structure. It is structure that is derived, versioned, and inspectable rather than frozen at capture by someone guessing what mattered. Provenance is first-class. The governance question shifts from "is the master data clean?" to "can we trace every decision?" — a question Karpa answers better.

"Regulated industries can't work this way."

Regulated industries need validated, structured artifacts — and Karpa produces exactly those as projections, with lineage attached. The change record, the batch record, the filing are generated against the constraints, not typed by a human hoping they match. The audit trail gets richer, not thinner.

"This eliminates jobs."

It eliminates data entry, and much of exception firefighting. The human role moves up the stack — from feeding the record to designing the system and owning judgment: fewer people reconciling reports, more people shaping the rules the agents operate under and mastering the exceptions only humans should. This is a real workforce change and deserves to be managed as one, not waved away.

"You can't rip out ERP."

Correct — and I am not proposing you try. Karpa begins beside your ERP, consuming from it, and assumes functions gradually. The ledger becomes a projection target. This is a strangler-fig migration, not a cutover — which is the subject of the next section.

XII — The bridge

A path you can actually walk

No one wakes up intent-native. The route is a sequence of horizons in which Karpa grows, the record shrinks to a rendered view, and the human role moves from running the system to designing it. At each step the organization is whole and shippable — never mid-cutover.

The horizon dates are a direction of travel, not a forecast. The order is the point: capture before orchestration, trust before autonomy, a shrinking record rather than a ripped-out one. The common roadmaps put capable agents on top of the record; this one takes the same trajectory one layer deeper — past agents on the record, to agents on intent.

XIII — Where I'd start

If I were starting today

You don't need to bet the company to begin — and you shouldn't. If I were starting today, these are the six moves I'd make first.

  1. Stop perfecting the schema; start capturing intent. The master-data program that never ends is sunk cost. Redirect that energy into standing up Karpa in one corridor and capturing how your people and customers actually communicate.
  2. Start where decision density, value, and volatility intersect. Begin where decisions are frequent and consequential — and, for manufacturing, where volatility is highest. Upstream sourcing — single-source, disruption-prone, high-consequence — is the ideal first corridor.
  3. Design around intent, not process. Don't digitize the current workflow. Rather than optimize the functional hand-offs you have, rebuild around what participants actually want, and let the process be derived.
  4. Make trust load-bearing from day one. Provenance, decision rights, and audit are not a later compliance phase. Trust is the currency of adoption, and the people-and-process barrier — the hardest part of scaling any of this — is paid down with it.
  5. Fund it from productivity while you build decision quality. Let the near-term efficiency of capture and ask-anything pay for the longer build toward orchestration — the two run in parallel, not in sequence.
  6. Re-skill record-keepers into system designers. Move your best people from feeding the ledger to shaping the rules the agents run under and mastering the exceptions. This is the workforce transition that decides whether the rest lands.

XIV — If I'm right

What I'd expect to see

I might be wrong about all of this. But if I'm right, the next few years should show it in ways I can be held to. So here is what I'd expect to see — written down and dated, so it can be checked against what actually happens.

  1. The master-data management function peaks and then declines. The "data steward" who guards the schema gives way to the "system designer" who shapes how intent is interpreted.
  2. At least one tier-one ERP vendor repositions as a projection engine — a system of record that sits beneath an intent layer, rendering the ledger on demand, rather than sitting above the business and dictating its shape.
  3. By 2030, a manufacturer runs a core sourcing corridor end to end without a planner ever opening a transaction screen — intent in, action out, humans on the exceptions.
  4. "Schema-on-write" becomes a legacy category of enterprise software, the way the on-premises mail server did — still running somewhere, no longer where the value is.
  5. Resilience becomes a continuously audited property rather than an annual binder; "dual-source readiness" appears as a disclosed operating metric, not a slide in a risk review.
  6. The interface itself goes physical. The first ambient, writable enterprise surface ships as hardware — a Karpa on the wall — and within a year no one finds it strange.

Coda

For fifty years we shaped the business to fit the database. It doesn't need us to anymore.

That is really the whole argument. The record stops being the master and becomes a view. People say what they mean, in the form that suits them, and the system does the translating it once forced on them. I don't think of this as a prediction so much as a direction — one I expect we will back into gradually, the way we backed into the cloud, long before anyone agreed it was happening.

I'm writing it down now, while it still sounds strange — because I don't believe it will for long. The constraints that made the old architecture necessary are already gone; the rest is a matter of time, and of who chooses to build it first.
